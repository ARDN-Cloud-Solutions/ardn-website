<p>Student information systems — the enrollment, attendance, grading, and records backbone of a K-12 district — are typically licensed against total enrollment, either as a per-student fee or a district-wide contract sized to student count. Enrollment is one of the few numbers a district doesn't control in the way it controls software adoption, which makes this pricing model climb almost automatically, year over year, independent of how deeply any given module actually gets used.</p>

<h2>Where the SIS bill actually comes from</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Enrollment-based core licensing.</strong> The base SIS contract scales with total student count district-wide, so growth in enrollment raises the bill even when the district's use of the system hasn't changed.</li>
  <li><strong>Module bundling beyond core records.</strong> Attendance and grading are core, but scheduling, state reporting, health records, and parent/student portals are frequently separate modules, each with its own pricing layered on the base contract.</li>
  <li><strong>Parent and student portal access.</strong> Self-service access for families and students to check grades, attendance, and schedules is sometimes bundled, sometimes a separate per-user or per-family add-on — worth confirming which model your contract actually uses.</li>
  <li><strong>State reporting compliance updates.</strong> SIS platforms handle state-mandated reporting formats that change periodically; keeping current with those changes is sometimes included, sometimes a maintenance fee on top of the base license.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why districts feel this differently than a business would</h2>
<p>A district can't reduce enrollment to cut its SIS bill the way a company might reduce seat count — the underlying number the contract is priced against isn't a discretionary headcount decision. That makes vendor selection and multi-year contract terms matter more here than in most per-seat software categories, since the core cost driver is largely outside the district's control once it's under contract.</p>

<h2>Where the fix actually lives</h2>
<p>The core SIS — enrollment, grading, attendance, state reporting — is the right place to keep a mature, compliance-focused vendor as system of record; that part of the bill isn't the place to look for savings. <h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Can a district switch SIS vendors mid-year?</h3>
<p>Rarely practically — SIS migrations are typically planned for a summer break or school-year boundary given the depth of enrollment, grading, and state-reporting data involved.</p>

<h3>Does a custom portal replace the state-mandated reporting features of an SIS?</h3>
<p>No — state reporting compliance should stay with the core SIS vendor whose product is built and maintained specifically for that requirement; a custom portal is additive, not a replacement for that function.</p>

<h3>Is per-student pricing negotiable?</h3>
<p>Multi-year district contracts sometimes have more room to negotiate than the sticker price suggests, particularly around module bundling — worth reviewing closely at each renewal cycle rather than auto-renewing.</p>
