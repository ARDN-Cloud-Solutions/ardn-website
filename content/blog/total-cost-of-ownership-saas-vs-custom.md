<p>Comparing a SaaS add-on's subscription price to a custom build's quote is comparing the wrong numbers. A proper total-cost-of-ownership (TCO) comparison has to include everything both paths cost over the life of the system — not just the number on the invoice or the proposal.</p>

<h2>What TCO includes on the SaaS side</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Per-seat license fees</strong> — the visible cost, and the one that scales directly with headcount.</li>
  <li><strong>Tier upgrades forced by feature needs</strong> — reaching one feature you need can mean paying the Enterprise-tier price on every seat, not just the one feature.</li>
  <li><strong>Integration and middleware costs</strong> — connecting the SaaS tool to everything else you run is rarely free or included.</li>
  <li><strong>Admin overhead</strong> — someone internally manages user provisioning, permissions, and vendor relationship on an ongoing basis.</li>
  <li><strong>Forced migrations</strong> — vendor-driven platform changes, deprecations, or acquisitions that force a re-implementation on their timeline, not yours.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What TCO includes on the custom-build side</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Build cost</strong> — a one-time or initial-phase investment to design and develop the system.</li>
  <li><strong>Ongoing operation and maintenance</strong> — hosting, updates, and support, ideally covered by a single flat fee rather than a separate line item per function.</li>
  <li><strong>Change requests</strong> — the cost of adapting the system as your process changes, which is a real ongoing cost on either path, just structured differently.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why the comparison usually gets run too late</h2>
<p>Most companies run this comparison only after a SaaS renewal shock, when the honest answer is that the crossover point should be checked before signing a multi-year contract, not after the third price increase. Building the TCO model at procurement time — even roughly — changes which contract terms are worth pushing back on.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Is a custom build always the lower-TCO option?</h3>
<p>No — for small, stable user counts with a standard process, a SaaS tool's TCO can be lower, especially in the near term. The framework here is for finding your actual crossover point, not asserting one answer fits every case.</p>

<h3>How far out should a TCO comparison project?</h3>
<p>Three to five years is a reasonable window for most mid-market decisions — long enough to capture headcount growth and at least one renewal cycle, short enough that the projection stays grounded.</p>

<h3>What's the single most commonly missed cost on the SaaS side?</h3>
<p>Admin overhead — the internal time spent on provisioning, permissions, and vendor management rarely gets counted as a cost of the tool, even though it's a real, ongoing labor cost tied directly to using it.</p>
