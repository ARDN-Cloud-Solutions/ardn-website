<p>Ask most mid-market Ops or IT leaders why their software spend keeps climbing, and headcount growth is usually the first answer — but it's rarely the whole story. Most companies run more tools than they think, most of those tools bill per seat, and the seat count on each one tends to only go up. That combination is what "seat sprawl" actually means, and it compounds quietly.</p>

<h2>How seat sprawl happens</h2>

<h3>Tool sprawl across departments</h3>
<p>Every department adopts its own tools over time — a CRM, a project management app, an HR platform, a support desk, a handful of niche point solutions. Each one is a reasonable decision in isolation. Collectively, they're a large and growing stack of per-seat subscriptions, several of which overlap in what they actually do.</p>

<h3>Default-to-full-access provisioning</h3>
<p>New hires are commonly given the same access as everyone else in their department by default, rather than the specific tier or tool their actual job requires. It's the fast way to onboard someone — and it means most orgs are provisioning more expensive access than most people use.</p>

<h3>Offboarding lag</h3>
<p>Seats that should be deactivated when someone leaves or changes roles often aren't, immediately or ever. Multiply a few weeks of lag per departure across a company with regular turnover, and it adds up to real recurring waste.</p>

<h3>External users on internal licenses</h3>
<p>Partners, vendors, and customers frequently end up provisioned on internal, full-price seats because it was the fastest way to grant them access — not because that's the right pricing tier for an outside user.</p>

<h2>Why mid-market companies feel this the most</h2>
<p>Small teams don't have enough tools or headcount for sprawl to matter much. Large enterprises typically have dedicated SaaS management functions specifically to control it. Mid-market companies (roughly 200–2,000 employees) tend to sit in the gap: enough tools and headcount growth to make sprawl expensive, but rarely a dedicated function whose job is watching it.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>How do we even find out how much seat sprawl we have?</h3>
<p>Start with a simple inventory: list every SaaS tool the company pays for, who owns it, and current seat count vs. active-user count for each. Most companies are surprised by how many tools show up once someone actually compiles the list.</p>

<h3>Isn't this just a procurement problem?</h3>
<p>Partly, but procurement controls new purchases — it doesn't fix seats that are already provisioned and quietly billing. Sprawl needs both tighter intake for new tools and an ongoing audit of what's already running.</p>

<h3>Does consolidating tools always save money?</h3>
<p>Usually, but not automatically — migration and change-management have real costs too. It's worth running the math per tool rather than assuming consolidation is always the right call; sometimes right-sizing seats within a tool is the faster win.</p>
