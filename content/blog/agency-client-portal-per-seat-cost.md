<p>Agencies giving clients visibility into project status, deliverables, or campaign reporting usually do it through the same project-management or reporting tool the internal team already uses — which means every client contact who gets a login is billed as a seat, on top of the internal team's own seats. Client counts are the one variable in an agency's business that's supposed to grow, which makes per-seat pricing on client access a direct tax on winning new accounts.</p>

<h2>Where the cost actually comes from</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Guest/collaborator seats still bill.</strong> Most project tools (Asana, monday.com, ClickUp) charge for guest or client seats at a discount from a full seat, but a discount is not zero — and it's still per-person, per-account.</li>
  <li><strong>Multiple contacts per client.</strong> Clients rarely have one contact who needs access — marketing lead, brand lead, and a finance approver on the same account can mean three seats for one client relationship.</li>
  <li><strong>Reporting dashboards add a second bill.</strong> A separate analytics/reporting tool with its own client-seat pricing often runs alongside the project tool, doubling the per-client cost.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The math scales against new business</h2>
<p>An agency landing new accounts is, by definition, adding client seats every time it grows — the opposite of the dormant-seat problem internal software audits usually chase. There's no "unused seat" to reclaim here; the growth itself is what drives the bill up.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Do clients need a full login to see project status?</h3>
<p>Not always — some agencies only need to expose a status view and file downloads, not full collaboration features. A custom portal can scope client access as narrowly or as fully as the relationship actually requires.</p>

<h3>Does this replace our internal project management tool?</h3>
<p>No — it sits alongside it, syncing client-visible data from whatever tool the team already runs internally. The internal tool stays the working system; the portal is the client-facing layer.</p>

<h3>Is white-labeling actually worth it beyond removing vendor branding?</h3>
<p>For client-facing work specifically, yes — a portal that looks like the agency's own product is a small but real part of the client experience the agency is selling, which a shared vendor tool with a logo swap doesn't fully deliver.</p>
